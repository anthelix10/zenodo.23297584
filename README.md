Data and code for: **Residual Renewable Cash and Reserve for Battery Revenue Floors**

Supporting research materials are available in https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23297584.

**Abstract**: We determine the residual renewable cash and the initial reserve required to maintain a target battery revenue floor. Only an agreed share of cash above a senior renewable threshold is transferred. Battery only and renewable backed contracts are assessed using the same ten-year cash sufficiency and expected margin tests. The Great Britain application combines a modelled 100 MW/200 MWh battery together with 940 matched days of wind and solar data. At 95% simulated path sufficiency, a £30,000/MW/year floor requires £10,403/MW/year under daily calculation or £10,588/MW/year under monthly netting, equivalent to 59.5% and 80.3% access. If a £1,500/MW reserve is introduced, the shares fall to 52.3% and 70.3%. In separate cases where higher shares are selected, the target is met in 296 and 279 out of 300 evaluation panels. Transferring the cash helps to reduce the difference between the daily and monthly floor amounts to £99/MW/year. The findings offer a risk management framework to quantify funding commitments.

**Interpretation and Scope**: The access reserve frontier translates a target payment into feasible funding commitments. Its use requires a defined renewable cash boundary and residual aggregation rule, followed by calculation of the cash required and a full access feasibility check. The cash scale then determines the access percentage. Central estimates should be distinguished from fixed terms evaluated on separate panels. Opening reserve and retention can be selected and priced as additional contract terms, but an economic objective is needed to choose a unique access reserve pair.
Giving the hurdle priority over a sweep does not guarantee a renewable payment. An asset specific application must use accessible cash and the actual settlement calendar in place of the assumptions. Annual reserve tests may miss intra-year shortfalls, while cross support rights and opening collateral have costs that are excluded from the reported gross floor capacities.
Resampling keeps battery, wind and solar observations from the same dates together. The 300 resampled datasets show how the results vary within this historical record. Even the higher monthly share, which is close to full access, fails to meet the target in some panels.

**Conclusion**: We use the same rules for both contracts to check whether cash can cover the payments and meet the required expected margin. This identifies how much renewable cash and opening reserve are needed to support the target battery revenue floor. In the reference application, a £30,000/MW/year floor requires approximately £10,400-£10,600/MW/year of transferred renewable cash. Central access estimates are 59.5% daily and 80.3% monthly, falling to 52.3% and 70.3% with £1,500/MW of opening reserve. Testing the same contract terms on separately resampled datasets shows that the estimated access percentages may not always support the target floor. When the daily and monthly arrangements transfer the same average amount of cash, their supported floors differ by only £99/MW/year. The study provides a method for calculating how much cash must be committed under an enforceable agreement to support a battery revenue floor.
