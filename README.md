Data and code for: **Residual Renewable Cash and Reserve for Battery Revenue Floors**

Supporting research materials are available in https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23297584.

**Abstract**: We develop a framework to size renewable cash commitments and initial reserves for battery energy storage system (BESS) revenue floors. Contracts are assessed using common ten-year cash sufficiency and expected margin tests. For a modelled 100 MW/200 MWh battery in Great Britain, a £30,000/MW/year floor requires renewable cash transfers of £7,896/MW/year under daily calculation or £8,848/MW/year under monthly netting. A wind power purchase agreement (PPA) examines how weak wind and battery revenue occurring at different times can improve combined payment guarantee capacity. Under selected profit and loss sharing terms, capacity exceeds summed standalone floor by 39.5% using the worst 5% path capacity measure and 35.3% under 95% ten-year sufficiency, without initial reserve. With £5,000/MW of reserve, selected gains reach 45.9% and 44.3%, respectively. Historical cash alignment explains part of these gains. The results connect cash availability, reserve and portfolio risk sharing within one floor sizing framework.

**Introduction**: Battery energy storage systems (BESSs) provide flexibility, but their merchant revenues are uncertain. The revenue floor offers guaranteed payment, provided that shortfalls can be funded. Existing research examines storage insurance contracts that compensate flexibility supplied to renewable producers [1], battery power purchase agreements (PPAs) that allocate battery revenues [2], and the effects of contract design on risk allocation and operating incentives [3]. Here, we ask how much renewable residual cash and initial reserve must be committed to support a chosen BESS revenue floor.
Pooling renewable revenues can improve portfolio risk return characteristics [4]. Common ownership of generation and storage do not, however, necessarily create value, and the outcome depends on market conditions and operating assumptions [5]. Cash committed to a battery reserve cannot simultaneously meet another obligation. Floor sizing must therefore distinguish the benefit of additional funding from the benefit of sharing risk across the available cash streams. Both depend on which cash the provider can access and when it becomes available.
Renewable PPA and battery operation offer a potential source of such risk sharing. The PPA retains fee income and trading gains or losses after paying the renewable asset owner, while the battery earns from buying and selling across price differences. Price movements associated with wind forecast revisions can reduce PPA profit while creating battery trading opportunities. When weak outcomes occur at different times, the combined account can absorb losses that would require separate funding if the exposures were assessed alone. The relevant question is whether this cash flow relationship improves the portfolio’s ability to meet fixed floor payments. 
We address these questions within a ten-year cash sufficiency and expected margin framework. The reference application determines the renewable residual commitment required for a target floor and how initial reserve changes that requirement. It compares daily and monthly residual allocation. A PPA examines participation in both trading profits and losses, comparing combined capacity with the sum of separately assessed BESS and renewable guarantee under the same total capital. Renewable participation, fee and reserve are varied under the 95% path sufficiency rule and a worst 5% path capacity measure. Correlations and an exclusion-of-2022 sensitivity describe the historical relationship. Together, these assessments connect renewable’s risk sharing value in supporting the battery floor.

**INTERPRETATION AND LIMITATION**: The residual cash application sizes a positive transfer to support a BESS floor, while the PPA application shares trading gains and losses and compares combined capacity with summed standalone limits. The two applications therefore answer different questions.
If the generation forecast rises while prices fall, we sell the extra energy below the DA price. If the forecast falls while prices rise, we buy back the shortfall above the DA price. The relevant risk is how changes in forecast volume move with changes in price.
When DA, WD and cashout prices change little, re-trading losses are small and more of the fee may remain as profit. Battery trading opportunities may be limited at the same time. Adverse price movements can reduce PPA profit while creating battery trading opportunities. The two income streams may therefore help offset each other.
Initial reserve and retention can be selected and priced as additional contract terms, but an economic objective is needed to choose a unique access reserve pair. Giving the hurdle priority over a sweep does not guarantee a renewable payment. An asset specific application must use accessible cash and the actual settlement calendar in place of the assumptions. Annual reserve tests may miss intra-year shortfalls, while cross support rights and initial collateral have costs that are excluded from the reported gross floor capacities.

**Conclusion**: The framework links battery floor sizing to the amount of renewable cash committed, the initial reserve and the way renewable and battery revenue perform together. A floor of £30,000/MW/year requires approximately £8,350/MW/year of transferred renewable cash. The required access shares are 52.4% under daily calculation and 70.8% under monthly netting, falling to 39.9% and 53.4% with £1,500/MW of initial reserve.
The PPA arrangement shows how negative cash flow dependence can contribute to higher guarantee capacity. When weak PPA outcomes coincide with stronger battery income, the combined account can absorb losses that would require separate funding if each exposure were assessed alone. Under the selected profit and loss sharing terms and without initial reserve, the worst 5% capacity rises from a summed standalone floor of £22,624 to £31,553/MW/year, a 39.5% pooling increase. A separately selected combination raises the 95% ten-year floor by 35.3%, against its summed standalone benchmark. With £5,000/BESS MW of initial reserve, the highest verified pooling gains within the tested ranges are 45.9% and 44.3%, respectively.
These results connect risk sharing directly to the original sizing problem that available cash provides funding, reserve carries that funding across periods, and cash flow correlation affects the floor payment guarantee capacity of the combined account. Historical alignment explains part of the pooling gain, while its magnitude varies with the exposure and contractual terms. Renewable backing should therefore be valued not only by how much cash it provides, but also by how effectively that cash and battery income support the same floor payment obligations.
